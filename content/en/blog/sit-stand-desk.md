The **sit-stand desk** has gone, in just a few years, from start-up gadget to a standard feature of office fit-outs. The principle is simple: a height-adjustable desktop that lets people work seated, then standing, and switch between the two during the day. Its real value, however, depends less on the furniture itself than on how it is set up and used. Here is what it delivers, what it does not, how to adjust it and how to choose one for a team.

## What a sit-stand desk is, and what it is not

A sit-stand desk, also called a height-adjustable desk or standing desk, is a workstation whose desktop moves up and down through a manual or motorised mechanism. Common adjustment ranges on the market run from about 60 to 65 cm at the lowest position up to 125 to 130 cm at the highest, which covers the seated position of a short person as well as the standing position of a tall one.

It should not be confused with a **fixed standing desk**, often a high table or bar-height counter, which imposes standing with no alternative. It also differs from a desktop converter placed on a standard desk, which raises the keyboard and screen but leaves a cluttered surface and often poor stability.

The key point fits in one idea: the goal is not to work standing, it is to **change position**. A sit-stand desk that stays in the low position all year is simply a more expensive standard desk.

## The real benefits of a sit-stand desk

The first benefit is tackling **sedentary work**. Sitting for long hours without a break is associated with musculoskeletal disorders, especially in the back and neck, as well as lower alertness by the end of the day. Being able to stand up without leaving the workstation breaks up those long motionless stretches.

The second benefit concerns **postural comfort**. Alternating positions spreads the load across different muscle groups and reduces the tension that builds up in the lower back. Many users also report more even energy levels throughout the day.

The third benefit is more organisational. Standing lends itself well to short calls, quick exchanges between colleagues or reviewing a document together. A desk that rises to conversation height encourages these interactions without tying up a room.

The limits exist and are worth stating. Standing too long is not the answer: prolonged standing tires the legs and feet and can in turn cause lower back pain. A sit-stand desk does not replace physical activity or breaks either. It is part of a broader look at workstation ergonomics, covered in the article on [office design trends 2026](/blog/office-design-trends-2026/).

## How to set the height of a sit-stand desk

A badly adjusted desk cancels out its own benefits. The reference points are the same seated and standing; only the desktop height changes.

### The elbow reference

Arms by the sides, elbows bent at about 90 degrees, the forearms should rest horizontally on the desktop, wrists straight and shoulders relaxed. Standing, the desktop height therefore roughly matches elbow height. A desktop that is too high lifts the shoulders; one that is too low rounds the back.

### Screen and line of sight

The screen sits at arm's length, generally between 50 and 70 cm, with its top edge at eye level or slightly below. When the desktop rises, the screen rises with it, which keeps the right viewing angle. A monitor arm makes fine adjustment easier, especially for a desk used by several people.

### Adjustments in the seated position

Sitting remains the most frequent position. Feet flat on the floor or on a footrest, thighs horizontal, back against the backrest: the desktop height is then set to the elbow. Chair and desk are adjusted together, never one without the other.

## How often to switch between sitting and standing

No universal rule sets an ideal ratio between sitting and standing time. Ergonomic recommendations do agree on one principle: **change position often**, usually every 30 to 60 minutes, rather than aiming for a number of hours on your feet.

Getting started should be gradual. Standing sessions of 15 to 20 minutes, two or three times a day, are enough for the first few weeks. The rhythm can then lengthen according to how it feels. Signals such as heavy legs or sore feet mean it is time to sit back down.

A few habits help make switching stick:

- link standing to specific tasks, such as calls or reading emails;
- save two memory presets to move from one position to the other at the press of a button;
- set a discreet reminder rather than relying on memory;
- move while standing, shifting weight from one leg to the other, rather than staying rigid.

This rhythm logic ties in with working-time methods: switching aligned with focus blocks is easier to sustain than an abstract instruction.

## Electric or manual: comparing mechanisms

The mechanism directly shapes how the desk is used. A desk that is a chore to adjust ends up stuck in a single position.

| Criterion | Manual crank | Electric single motor | Electric dual motor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position change | 30 seconds to 1 minute of effort | A few seconds | A few seconds |
| Memory presets | No | Often | Yes, generally |
| Stability at full height | Fair | Variable | Good |
| Load capacity | Limited | Medium | High |
| Price level | Lowest | Mid-range | Highest |
| Recommended use | Occasional use | Individual desk | Intensive use, shared desk |

For daily use in a company, the electric model is almost always the right choice, because it is the speed of the height change that guarantees switching. A dual motor is justified for wide desktops, heavy loads with several screens and shared desks that change height several times a day.

## Criteria for choosing a sit-stand desk for a company

Beyond the mechanism, five criteria separate good equipment from a regretted purchase.

1. The **adjustment range**, which must cover the shortest and tallest intended users, seated and standing.
2. **Stability at full height**: a desktop that wobbles while typing quickly becomes unbearable. T-shaped or C-shaped frames with a reinforcing crossbar are preferable.
3. **Desktop size**, generally 120 to 180 cm wide, chosen according to the number of screens and the available floor space.
4. **Cable management**, essential since the whole workstation moves up and down: under-desk tray, vertical cable spine and a power strip fixed to the desktop.
5. **Safety and warranty**: collision detection to avoid pinching, a long warranty on the motors and availability of spare parts.

In terms of budget, entry-level new models start at around 200 euros, while professional electric models mostly sit between 600 and 1,500 euros depending on stability, desktop and warranty. Simple accessories complete the set-up: an anti-fatigue mat for standing, a footrest for sitting, a monitor arm.

## Integrating sit-stand desks into an office layout

Equipping a team is not just a matter of replacing tables. A sit-stand desk changes the space: in the high position it breaks sight lines and alters the acoustics of an open floor. In a dense open space, the mix of high and low desks should be anticipated from the floor plan stage, as the comparison of [private office vs open space](/blog/private-office-vs-open-space/) points out.

In a **flex office**, where several people use the same desk, it must be adjustable quickly and without hesitation. A height display, memory presets and visible instructions on the desk prevent it from staying frozen at the last occupant's height. The pitfalls specific to this way of working are detailed in the [flex office guide](/blog/flex-office-guide/).

A gradual roll-out often works better than a complete replacement: equip part of the desks first, observe actual use over a few weeks, then adjust the number and placement. The equipment also supports efforts to get employees to [return to the office](/blog/return-to-office/), provided it comes with explanations of the correct settings.
